I. The ramp

The whole day, sampled off two physical curves

The warm half comes off the Planckian locus — the chromaticity a blackbody actually radiates, which is what an incandescent bulb, a candle and a low sun all approximate. The cool half comes off the CIE daylight locus, which is the correct curve for sky light and sits slightly off the blackbody line. Dusk comes off neither: it is Mie and Rayleigh scattering, so it is sampled from measured sunset chromaticity and labelled honestly as such.

Lightness is pinned; only hue and chroma move. That is the whole trick. Ink sits at L 0.500 at 03:00 and at L 0.500 at 20:00, so the drift cannot cost contrast. Measured across all 24 hours the timestamp holds a minimum of 5.67:1 on paper and 5.08:1 on its chip in light theme, 8.41:1 and 7.21:1 in dark. There is no hour at which this palette is less readable than another.

II. The idea

Two registers on one wheel, separated by chroma

A palette that assigns hue by time has an obvious problem: it collides with a palette that assigns hue by meaning. If 6 a.m. is blue and broken is blue, the interface is lying twice.

The resolution is to stop treating the colour wheel as one namespace. Time lives at chroma 0.002–0.048. It is a tint you feel rather than read — paper, chips, hairlines, the timestamp itself. Meaning lives at chroma 0.145–0.170, loud and fixed, and never moves with the clock. Between them sits a third tier at 0.075 for machine-collected data.

The gap between the registers is not decorative. It is 3× the width of the entire time register, so no hour of the day can ever be mistaken for a habit state.

III. Identity & semantics

Four accents. Two of them are the same colour on purpose.

The primary of this app is its kept-signal. That is a deliberate collapse of two roles: a life-tracker exists to accumulate kept days, so the brand colour and the success colour should not compete for the user's attention. Lamplight is the interface's amber — buttons, focus rings, the caret — and it is also what a kept habit looks like.

Every value below was computed in OKLCH and gamut-fitted by bisection on chroma — 64 iterations, holding L and H fixed, until the sRGB triplet lands inside [0, 1]. One colour needed it: lamplight ink was requested at C 0.125 and clipped to the sRGB boundary. Everything else was already in gamut.

IV. Neutrals

No pure grey anywhere in the system

The light theme's neutrals carry hue 64 — a trace of lamplight, so paper reads as paper. The dark theme's carry hue 262 — a trace of twilight sky, because at 2 a.m. the ground should be the colour of the room, not the colour of the lamp. This is the one place the two themes take their tint from different ends of the axis, and it is why dark is re-derived rather than inverted.

Light — ground oklch(0.982 0.006 64)

Dark — ground oklch(0.190 0.013 262)

Dark ground is oklch(0.190 0.013 262) = #11141A, not black. Sunken surfaces bottom out at L 0.158. Nothing in the system reaches #000, so an OLED panel never has to switch pixels fully off and back — no smear on scroll, which matters for an app opened in bed.

VI. The binary that matters

Kept / broken, and why it is not green and red

Roughly one man in twelve has a red–green deficiency. Okabe & Ito's reference palette works by separating colours in brightness and saturation while stepping around the yellow–green confusion band — and the pair it leans on hardest is blue against orange. That pair is already the two ends of a colour-temperature axis, which is the happy accident this whole direction is built on: the most colour-blind-safe axis available and the physical axis of daylight are the same axis.

Colour is never load-bearing here. Kept is a filled disc with a check. Broken is an open ring with a slash. Pending is a dashed open ring with nothing inside. Fill, stroke style and interior mark all differ, so the strip reads correctly in greyscale, in a screenshot, and for anyone who cannot see the hue at all.

VII. Simulation

Measured under three dichromacies

Simulated with the Viénot–Brettel–Mollon matrices applied in linear RGB, not gamma-encoded sRGB — applying them to gamma values is the common mistake and it flatters the result. ΔE is Euclidean distance in OKLab; below about 0.10 a pair is confusable at a glance. The shaded row is the naive green/red baseline this palette is arguing against.

The naive pair loses 82% of its separation under deuteranopia and lands at ΔE 0.056 — two muddy olives, functionally the same colour. Kelvin Drift's pair gains a little under deuteranopia (102%), because collapsing the red–green channel pushes amber toward yellow and away from blue. Its worst case is protanopia at 84% retained, which is still ΔE 0.332 — above the pair's normal-vision separation in the baseline.

VIII. Honest failure

Where this palette is weak

ΔE 0.139
Pending against passive, under deuteranopia. This is the weakest pair in the set and it is genuinely below the comfortable threshold. It survives only because the two never appear as alternatives: pending is a habit state on a glyph, passive is a data source on a dotted leader. If they ever land in the same column, the glyph has to carry it entirely.
ΔE 0.173
Kept against pending/warning. Amber and rose are neighbours on the warm end of my own axis, and deuteranopia narrows the gap further. Mitigated by form rather than hue: kept is filled, pending is hollow, so the comparison is never fill-versus-fill.
2.10:1
Lamplight fill is not text. At oklch(0.762 0.156 64) on paper it reaches 2.10:1 — nowhere near AA. It is a fill, a rule and a 2px focus ring only. Amber text uses lamplight ink at 5.49:1, which is a visibly darker, browner colour. Any design that shows you one amber and quietly uses it for both is not measuring.
tritan
Tritanopia is the axis this palette is built on. Blue against yellow is exactly what tritanopia attacks, so on paper this should be the worst case. It is not — 92–98% retained — but only because ΔL is 0.333, doing nearly all the work. If a future screen ever puts kept and broken at the same lightness, this collapses. The lightness gap is a hard constraint, not a stylistic preference.
subtle
The drift is nearly invisible on paper. Ground chroma tops out at 0.033 — #F6F9FF at 3 a.m. against #FFF8F2 at 6 p.m. On a phone in sunlight most people will never consciously notice. That is intended: the ramp above amplifies it three ways so you can audit the mechanism, but in the app it is a mood, not a readout. If you want it legible rather than felt, raise the ink multiplier, not the paper.

IX. Reasoning

How I got here

I started from the data model, not from a mood board. Every row in this app has a timestamp; almost nothing else is guaranteed. Category is optional, habits are inferred, screen time arrives from elsewhere. Time is the one field that is always present and always precise — so it is the only field that can safely carry a global visual encoding. Colouring by category would fail the moment the user types a line that fits no category, which the sample log does five times out of five.

Then I looked for a physical source rather than inventing one. A hue ramp I designed by eye would be arbitrary and I would have no principled answer to "why is 4 p.m. that colour?". Blackbody radiation and the CIE daylight locus give real chromaticities for real light, and they meet near 6500 K at almost exactly zero chroma. That neutral crossing is a gift: it means noon is genuinely colourless and the ramp has a natural rest point instead of a seam.

The interpolation is done in OKLab a/b, not in hue. Interpolating hue from 258° to 80° has to travel through either green or magenta, and both look wrong at mid-morning. Interpolating the rectangular coordinates passes straight through the origin, so the tint fades to true neutral around 10:30 and comes back out the warm side. That is also what actually happens to light. The mechanism and the physics agree, which is usually a sign the mechanism is right.

The hard constraint arrived third. Kept/broken has to survive dichromacy, and the canonical safe pair is blue against orange. I had already committed to a warm–cool temperature axis for unrelated reasons. Rather than treat that as luck, I made it structural: the semantic pair sits at the two ends of the same axis the clock walks along, just three times further out in chroma. One axis, two amplitudes.

Restraint was the last decision and the most important one. The first version of this ramp had twelve visible hues across the day and looked like a weather app. The fix was to cap ground chroma at 0.033 and let the ramp cross true neutral for four hours in the middle. What is left is a two-pole axis with one genuine third accent at dusk — not a rainbow, a thermometer.

What makes this different from the other three proposals: they assign colour by meaning, which produces a fixed palette you memorise. This one assigns colour by meaning and by time, on separate saturation registers, which produces a palette that is partly a clock. Open the app at 2 a.m. and it is a measurably different, cooler object than at 6 p.m. — without a single semantic colour having moved, and without a single contrast ratio having changed. For an app one person opens every day, often late at night, on their own data, that ambient signal is worth more than another set of category chips.
